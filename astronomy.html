How the Astronomy & Sightline Checks Work

Most solar eclipses happen high in the sky, where any open backyard or park gives a great view. The 2028 Otago total eclipse is different: it happens late on a mid-winter afternoon, when the sun is sitting low in the north-west. Here is how this app works out what you will see—and whether a hill will be in your way.

Independent, unvalidated tool — technical reviewers welcome

This is an independent project developed with AI assistance and has not yet been independently validated by an astronomer or surveyor. This page explains the concepts in plain language; for the full mathematical specification—ephemeris series, coordinate frames, ΔT, refraction equations, and error budgets—read the technical document.

1. Why this eclipse is all about the skyline

On 22 July 2028, the Moon's dark central shadow (the umbra) sweeps across Fiordland, Queenstown, Central Otago, and Dunedin between 4:14 PM and 4:19 PM NZST. Because it is the middle of winter, the sun is already sinking toward sunset in the north-west.

Sun height at totality

8° – 10° high

About one fist held at arm's length above the horizon

Direction to look

North-West (~311°)

Toward the Southern Alps and inland ranges

Totality duration

Up to 2m 59s

Longest near the centreline; shorter near the edges

A handy field rule: hold your clenched fist out at arm's length with the bottom of your fist on the horizon. The top of your fist is roughly 10° up. If a mountain ridge to the north-west rises higher than your fist, it will hide the totally eclipsed sun. That is why Queenstown and Arrowtown town centres cannot see totality—Ben Lomond and the surrounding peaks rise 15°–21° in the exact direction of the sun—while open basins like Cromwell look across lower horizons and see it clearly.

2. Predicting the Sun and Moon for your exact spot

Standard 3D map engines have a built-in sun and moon for drawing shadows, but their lunar orbits are simplified to save computer work—during testing, the 3D globe's built-in moon missed totality by several minutes and only covered 88% of the sun.

Instead, this app uses a dedicated astronomical library (astronomy-engine) based on high-precision solar and lunar orbital models developed at the Paris Observatory (VSOP87 and ELP/MPP02). Whenever you pick a location on the map, the app calculates the eclipse specifically for that exact coordinate and elevation:

1

Your position on a curved, spinning Earth

Because the Moon is relatively close to Earth (~373,000 km on eclipse day), viewing it from different towns in Otago changes the angle slightly (parallax). Queenstown reaches totality about 42 seconds earlier than Dunedin, and a spot near the centreline gets nearly 3 minutes of totality compared to under a minute near the edge.

2

The size of the Sun and Moon in the sky

On 22 July 2028, the Moon appears about 4.5% wider than the Sun (32.9 arcminutes vs 31.5 arcminutes—just over half a degree across). The app calculates their exact overlap on every tick of the clock to show how much of the sun's surface is covered (obscuration).

3

Why the eclipse ends below the horizon

After totality finishes around 4:18 PM, the Moon takes another hour to slide completely off the Sun. By the time the eclipse finishes around 5:19 PM, the sun has already set on the east coast (Dunedin and Oamaru) and is less than 1.7° high everywhere else. The app only judges a spot on moments when the sun is actually above the horizon.

3. Tracing your sightline across 45 km of terrain

When you click Can I see it? (or select a spot from the catalogue), the app stands 1.7 metres (average eye height) above the ground at your chosen spot and marches a fan of virtual sightlines outward toward the sun's direction, sampling elevation data from 100 metres out to 45 kilometres away.

To get an accurate answer over tens of kilometres, a simple straight-line calculation on a flat map isn't enough. The sightline check accounts for four real-world effects:

Effect What happens physically How the app handles it
Earth's curvature The Earth curves away beneath distant mountains, making a peak 30 km away sit about 70 metres lower than on a flat plane. Applies Earth curvature drop along great-circle bearings out to 45 km.
Atmospheric refraction Air is denser near the ground and bends light downward. This lifts the apparent position of an 8° sun by ~0.11° (about a fifth of the sun's width) and also slightly lifts distant mountain ridges. Applies standard astronomical refraction to the sun and terrestrial refraction (k = 0.13) to distant terrain.
Width of the Sun's disc The sun isn't a single point of light—its disc spans ~0.52° (radius ~0.26°). If the sun's centre barely clears a ridge by 0.1°, its bottom edge is still behind the hill. Flags clearances under 0.26° as grazing so you know to move slightly higher for the full disc.
Sun movement during the eclipse Between the start of the eclipse (3:09 PM) and sunset, the sun moves ~26° sideways across the sky while sinking lower. Tests the start, peak, and end of the eclipse—and checks whether a spot stays clear for the entire duration of totality.

4. Why darkness arrives all at once (and eye safety)

If you use the Watch it happen button or drag the timeline slider, you will notice that the 3D landscape stays relatively bright until more than 85% of the sun is covered, and then plunges into twilight in the final minute before totality.

That matches how human eyes experience a real eclipse. Daylight is so bright that your pupils open up to compensate as the moon covers the first 80–90% of the sun—the light feels slightly eerie and silvery, but still like daytime. Only when the last few percent of the sun's surface disappears does the light collapse.

Eye safety rule: Even when 99.9% of the sun is covered, the remaining tiny sliver of the sun's surface is still tens of thousands of times brighter than the corona and can permanently damage your eyes. Certified eclipse glasses (ISO 12312-2) must be worn for the entire partial eclipse, and may only come off during the brief window of 100% totality when the sun's bright disc is completely hidden behind the moon.

5. What the elevation model cannot see

The satellite elevation model used by the app measures terrain height on a ~30-metre grid (sharpened to ~7 metres around the four preset towns). That is ideal for mountains, hills, and valley walls several kilometres away, but it has important blind spots right next to where you stand:

  • • Trees, shelterbelts, and buildings: Because the sun is only 8°–10° high, a 15-metre row of pine trees 90 metres away is tall enough to block it. The app ignores the first 100 metres around you so elevation noise doesn't create false alarms.
  • • Road cuttings and embankments: A roadside pull-off cut into a bank on its north-west side can block a view that looks clear from the middle of the valley.
  • • Private land and winter access: The catalogue finds spots within 100 metres of mapped roads and tracks, but proximity on OpenStreetMap is not legal permission, and high-country 4WD tracks in July may be snowed in or closed.
