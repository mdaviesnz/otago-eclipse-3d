Astronomical & Geodetic Methods

This specification describes how Otago Eclipse 3D computes solar and lunar positions, topocentric eclipse circumstances, atmospheric refraction, and terrain horizon obstruction for the total solar eclipse of 22 July 2028 over the South Island of New Zealand. It is written for domain reviewers in astronomy and geodesy and states all physical assumptions, numerical algorithms, and uncertainty budgets explicitly.

1. Physical Context of the Event

On the afternoon of 22 July 2028 (Southern Hemisphere winter), the lunar umbra crosses Fiordland, Southland, and Otago from west-north-west to east-south-east before leaving the New Zealand coast just after 16:19 NZST (04:19 UTC):

  • Maximum eclipse in Dunedin (-45.8788°, 170.5028°): 04:17:20.5 UTC (16:17:20.5 NZST).
  • Apparent solar altitude at maximum: 8.37° (Dunedin, east coast) to 10.0°–10.8° (Queenstown / Fiordland, inland west).
  • Solar azimuth at maximum: 310.6°–312.9° (north-west).
  • Totality duration on the centreline: up to 179 s (2m 59s).
  • Sunset during the closing partial phase: Fourth contact (C4) occurs with the Sun below 1.7° altitude across the entire South Island, and slightly below the horizon along the east coast (Dunedin refracted altitude −0.09°, geometric altitude −0.63° at 05:19:58 UTC).

Because the Sun sits only 8°–10° above the north-western horizon at totality across a region of steep glacial valleys and basin-and-range topography, local terrain obstruction is the primary determinant of visibility. At these grazing altitudes, atmospheric refraction and Earth curvature are comparable to the solar angular diameter (~0.52°) and must be modelled consistently.

2. Ephemeris and Coordinate Pipeline

2.1 Ephemeris Engine

All solar and lunar positions and eclipse contact times come from astronomy-engine (Don Cross, MIT License), used identically across two independent code paths:

EnvironmentPackagePurpose
Browser (simulator.js) astronomy.browser.min.js (2.1.19) Live topocentric positions, continuous magnitude & obscuration, per-location contact times
Offline pipeline (precompute/build_viewpoints.py) astronomy-engine (PyPI, 2.1.19) 0.1° regional circumstance grid (EclipseField), POI/grid clearances, preset profiles
  • Solar theory: VSOP87 (Bretagnon & Francou 1988).
  • Lunar theory: ELP/MPP02-derived series (Chapront & Francou 2003).
  • Precession & nutation: IAU 2000B model (McCarthy & Luzum 2003).

Epoch accuracy vs. multi-century bound: While astronomy-engine documents a conservative worst-case bound of ±1 arcminute across several centuries, its actual relative Sun–Moon positional accuracy in the 2028 epoch is ~1–3 arcseconds. Since the Moon's topocentric synodic motion is ~0.55″/s, a 1′ along-track error would displace contact times by ~110 s; in validation (§8), astronomy-engine with its default ΔT matches published Fred Espenak / NASA GSFC predictions at Dunedin to within 0–3 s.

Exclusion of CesiumJS's internal ephemeris: CesiumJS (v1.118) is used strictly as a 3D terrain renderer. Its internal Simon1994PlanetaryPositions lunar approximation is intended for scene lighting only and was measured during development to miss totality at Dunedin (~88% peak obscuration, several minutes late).

2.2 Coordinate Transformations

For an observer at WGS84 geodetic coordinates (φ, λ, h) at instant t:

eq = Equator(body, t, observer, ofdate=true, aberration=true) hz = Horizon(t, observer, eq.ra, eq.dec, Refraction.Normal)
  • Equator(..., ofdate=true, aberration=true) evaluates J2000.0 state vectors, applies annual aberration and light-time, rotates to the true equator and equinox of date via IAU 2000B precession/nutation, and subtracts the observer's geocentric WGS84 vector (including diurnal aberration) to yield topocentric (α, δ) and topocentric distance d in AU.
  • Horizon(..., Refraction.Normal) converts topocentric (α, δ) via local apparent sidereal time (GAST) into local azimuth A (clockwise from true North) and refracted apparent altitude αapp.

3. Time Scales and ΔT

  • Internal time: UTC throughout. Displayed wall-clock times are NZST = UTC + 12h (New Zealand winter; NZDT = UTC + 13h does not apply in July).
  • UT → TT conversion: astronomy-engine converts UT to Terrestrial Time (TT = UT + ΔT). Left alone it extrapolates ΔT with the Espenak & Meeus (2006) polynomial, which gives 76.65 s on 22 July 2028. Earth has been rotating faster than that fit expected, and measured values put 2028 near 69 s, so both code paths override the library with a fixed ΔT = 69.2 s (DELTA_T_SECONDS in eclipse-time.js and precompute/eclipse.py).
  • Effect of the override: Compared with the library default, every contact in the region is 8.6–9.5 s later and both limits of the path of totality lie ~1.8 km further south. Near the limits that changes how long totality lasts: Hampden goes from 105 s to 101 s, Te Anau from 46 s to 56 s.
  • Sensitivity: Each 1 s of ΔT moves contact times by ~1.2 s and the path limits by ~0.25 km. The adopted value is a prediction, not a measurement; an error of a second in it is well inside what this tool needs.

4. Observer Geodesy and Elevation Sensitivity

The observer is constructed as Observer(latitude, longitude, height) with WGS84 latitude/longitude and h = hterrain + 1.7 m eye height.

  • Ellipsoidal vs. orthometric height datum: Re:Earth Terrain (Copernicus GLO-30 / Mapterhorn) is referenced to the WGS84 ellipsoid, whereas Observer.height is documented as elevation above mean sea level. Over Otago, the NZGeoid2016 separation is N ≈ +5 m to +35 m. For terrain horizon checks (§7), both hobs and hterrain(d) come from the same DEM, so the datum offset cancels in hterrain − hobs.
  • Shadow-axis grazing sensitivity: Because the lunar shadow arrives at α☉ ≈ 8.4°–10°, raising an observer by Δh shifts their intercept with the inclined shadow cone down-track by Δx = Δh cot α☉ ≈ 6.8 Δh.

5. Eclipse Circumstances, Magnitude, and Obscuration

5.1 Local Contact Times

Per-observer contact times (C1, C2, maximum, C3, C4) come from SearchLocalSolarEclipse(startTime, observer). In the offline catalogue pipeline, EclipseField evaluates a 0.1° × 0.1° grid (~2,000 searches) and interpolates solar altitude, azimuth, and duration bilinearly. The interpolated duration only ranks candidates: duration falls to zero along a curve at the edge of the path, where interpolation ran up to 20 s short, so each entry that is written gets its own SearchLocalSolarEclipse at its position and eye height, and that figure is stored. Totality is enforced as a strict 4-corner boundary: all four surrounding grid nodes must lie inside totality so edge candidates are never optimistically included.

5.2 Continuous Magnitude, Obscuration, and Totality Criterion (eclipseAt)

From topocentric distances d☉, d☾ (converted from AU via 1 AU = 149,597,870.7 km):

s_sun = asin(R_sun / d_sun), R_sun = 695 700 km (IAU nominal photospheric radius) s_moon = asin(R_moon / d_moon), R_moon = 1 737.4 km (IAU mean lunar radius, k0 = 0.2725076) sep = acos(clamp(u_sun · u_moon, -1, 1)) mag = max(0, (s_sun + s_moon - sep) / (2 · s_sun)) obsc = A_overlap(s_sun, s_moon, sep) / (π · s_sun²) isTotal = (s_moon >= s_sun) and (sep <= s_moon - s_sun)

Strict totality gating: An uncovered solar crescent of 0.1% area (obsc = 0.999) occurs when the lunar limb is still ~16.6″ short of internal tangency (~33 s before C2 or after C3) and remains blindingly bright. The UI therefore gates the Totality readout, time-left countdown, and "safe to look without eclipse glasses" narration strictly on geometric internal tangency (isTotal), and caps displayed partial obscuration at 99.9% whenever !isTotal.

Smooth-sphere approximation: No Kaguya/LRO lunar limb profile is applied (±1–3 s effect on C2/C3). Standard Besselian predictions (Espenak & Meeus 2006) also adopt a smaller lunar valley radius (k = 0.272281 R⊕ ≈ 1,735.95 km, ~0.8″ smaller than the IAU mean radius) for umbral contacts, making astronomy-engine's smooth-sphere totality duration ~2–3 s longer.

6. Atmospheric and Terrestrial Refraction

Both the live simulator and the offline catalogue apply a unified refraction model across solar altitude and terrain curvature:

6.1 Astronomical Refraction of the Sun (Refraction.Normal)

astronomy-engine applies Bennett's (1982) formula for standard conditions (P = 1010 hPa, T = 10°C):

R_astro (arcmin) = 1.02 / tan(alt_geom + 10.3 / (alt_geom + 5.11))

At Dunedin maximum eclipse, this lifts the geometric Sun from 8.2642° to 8.3712° (a lift of +0.1070° ≈ +6.42′).

6.2 Terrestrial Refraction of the Horizon (k = 0.13)

Near-horizontal rays from distant mountain ridges are bent downward by the lower atmosphere's vertical density gradient. Adopting standard geodetic refraction coefficient k = 0.13 gives an effective Earth radius:

R_eff = R_earth / (1 - k) = 6 371 000 / 0.87 ≈ 7 322 989 m drop(d) = (1 - k) · d² / (2 · R_earth) = d² / (2 · R_eff)

This lifts distant terrain relative to airless geometry by Δθterr ≈ (k · d) / (2 R⊕) (+0.35′ at 10 km, +1.05′ at 30 km, +1.58′ at 45 km).

6.3 Cancellation of Winter Basin Inversion Anomalies

In a July late-afternoon Otago basin, cold-air pooling and temperature inversions (dT/dz > 0) can raise local boundary-layer k above 0.13. However, by Snell's law and optical reversibility, any two rays arriving at the observer at the same apparent elevation angle trace the exact same curved path through the lower atmosphere. A solar ray skimming a ridge at distance d traverses the final segment d through the same basin air mass as light from the ridge itself. Anomalous bending below the ridge height therefore adds equally to α☉,app and θterr,app, cancelling out in the net clearance Δα = α☉,app − θterr,app.

7. Terrain Horizon Ray-Marching

d_{n+1} = d_n + max(25 m, 0.03 · d_n), d ∈ [100 m, 45 000 m] (161 samples/ray) angle(d) = atan2(h_terrain(d) - h_observer - d² / (2 · R_eff), d) horizon = max_d angle(d), clearance = alt_sun - horizon
  • Inner cutoff (dmin = 100 m): Chosen from the ~30 m DEM vertical uncertainty (σz ≈ 5 m), which produces an angular noise floor arctan(σz / d) of ±32° at 8 m, ±5.7° at 50 m, and ±2.9° at 100 m (~3.3 pixels).
  • Great-circle geodesics: Both code paths trace true great-circle bearings (via the local ENU tangent frame in JS and spherical forward geodesics in Python).
  • Solar centre vs. lower limb (s☉ ≈ 0.26°): Because clearance is measured from the solar centre, clearances in (0°, 0.26°) mean the lower limb of the solar/lunar disc intersects the ridge and are explicitly flagged as grazing.
  • Dual-resolution preset profiles: The five town presets use zoom 13 (~6.8 m/px) within 9 km and zoom 11 (~27 m/px) out to 45 km across a 1°-stepped azimuth profile. This reduced worst-case raster-vs-mesh error to 0.13° across the 12 town/contact bearings measured for Queenstown, Arrowtown, Cromwell and Dunedin; Wānaka was added later with the same method and was not part of that comparison.

9. Meteorological Climatology & Cloud Cover

While terrain horizon ray-marching establishes where the low solar disc is physically unobstructed by local relief, atmospheric cloud cover is the definitive real-world determinant of visibility. To provide quantitative guidance on where clear skies are historically most probable, the app incorporates a 48-year satellite climate data record across the South Island totality path.

9.1 Data Source: EUMETSAT CM SAF CLARA-A3

Cloud statistics are derived from the CLARA-A3 (CM SAF cLoud, Albedo and Surface Radiation dataset, Edition 3) and its operational Interim Climate Data Record (ICDR), produced by the EUMETSAT Satellite Application Facility on Climate Monitoring (CM SAF) (DOI: 10.5676/EUM_SAF_CM/CLARA_AVHRR/V003).

  • Sensor: Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer (AVHRR) onboard NOAA and MetOp polar-orbiting satellites.
  • Temporal baseline: 48 consecutive July months (1979 to 2026). July is isolated to match the seasonal winter synoptic circulation of the 22 July event.
  • Spatial resolution: 0.25° × 0.25° (~20 km × 28 km at 45°S), covering the regional bounding box (43.5°S–47.0°S, 166.0°E–172.0°E).
  • Primary variables:
    • cfc_day: Daytime Fractional Cloud Cover (0–100%), filtered for daytime satellite passes.
    • cfc: Total 24-hour Fractional Cloud Cover (0–100%).
    • cfc_low: Low-level Cloud Fractional Cover, capturing boundary-layer stratus and valley fog pools.

9.2 Mathematical Formulation & Probability Metrics

For each grid point (φ, λ), the multi-decadal climatological mean daytime cloud fraction is computed across the N = 48 July months:

C_day(φ, λ) = (1 / N) · ∑_{y=1979}^{2026} CFC_day,y(φ, λ) P_clear(φ, λ) = 100% - C_day(φ, λ)

For an arbitrary observer coordinate (φobs, λobs), the values are interpolated bilinearly from the surrounding four 0.25° grid nodes. In the catalogue and spot details:

  • Historic July daytime cloud cover: Cday (percentage of sky obscured on average during July afternoons).
  • Clear-sky / favourable probability: Pclear = 100% − Cday (empirical frequency of open sky).
  • Low cloud / fog risk: Flagged whenever low-level cloud cover Clow > 18%, highlighting locations vulnerable to winter ground inversions or coastal marine advection.

9.4 Low-Altitude Slant-Path Geometry

Standard satellite cloud fraction represents the nadir (vertical zenith) view. Because the eclipsed Sun sits at an altitude of only α ≈ 8.4°–10.0° toward azimuth 311° (north-west), the sightline traverses an optical airmass of:

m ≈ 1 / sin(α) ≈ 1 / sin(8.4°) ≈ 6.85 airmasses

Consequently, an observer looks through nearly seven times more atmospheric path length than zenith. Cloud banks banked against the Main Divide 40–80 km to the north-west can obscure the solar disc even when the local zenith sky overhead appears mostly clear. This reinforces the advantage of inland viewing sites positioned well downwind of the Main Divide.
